# Case 07: Delayed Colorectal Cancer Diagnosis Due to Multi-Service Pathway Failures

**Source**: HDC NZ Decision 25HDC00796 (Health and Disability Commissioner, 23 February 2026)
**Event Type**: Clinical deterioration / diagnostic delay
**SAC Equivalent**: SAC 1 (stage 4 cancer diagnosis — poor prognosis)
**Difficulty**: 3 — Complex

## Case Narrative

[Mr B], a 56-year-old man, presented to his GP in October 2023 with intermittent rectal bleeding and low-grade anaemia. Following a declined gastroenterology referral, an incorrect administrative triage, and an eight-month wait complicated by system-level capacity failures, [Mr B] was ultimately diagnosed with stage 4 colorectal cancer in June 2024. The Health and Disability Commissioner found Health NZ | Te Whatu Ora Southern breached Right 4(1) of the Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers' Rights.

**Initial GP Presentation and Referral Declined (October 2023)**

On 2 October 2023, [Mr B] consulted his GP [Dr A] reporting three to four months of intermittent rectal bleeding and low-grade anaemia without iron deficiency. He also had urinary retention and poor urinary stream, being evaluated by Urology for a prostate condition requiring surgical intervention.

[Dr A] referred [Mr B] to gastroenterology. Importantly, the referral documented the bleeding and a previously normal digital rectal examination but did not mention bowel habit changes, weight loss, reduced appetite, or tenesmus.

On 4 October 2023, the gastroenterology service declined this referral, categorising the bleeding as "outlet bleeding" which often resolves spontaneously. The adverse event review (AER) conducted by Health NZ later noted that the gastroenterology service could have sought symptom clarification from [Dr A] before declining — and could have directly transferred the referral to general surgery — potentially shortening the diagnostic pathway by eight weeks.

**Re-referral and Administrative Error (December 2023)**

On 14 December 2023, [Mr B] returned to [Dr A] with ongoing bleeding now described as "quite a bit coming out," blood-stained water in the toilet, and loose bowel movements. [Dr A] documented changed bowel patterns and worsening anaemia. Repeat digital rectal examinations remained normal. [Dr A] referred [Mr B] to general surgery.

The surgical service accepted the referral, noting the clinical picture suggested "outlet bleeding but anaemic and age requires sigmoidoscopy at least." [Mr B] was triaged as semi-urgent.

A critical administrative error then occurred: staff set the treat-by date to 16 June 2024 using a 120-working-day timeframe rather than the correct 56-working-day standard for semi-urgent cases. This single administrative error extended [Mr B]'s expected wait by approximately 64 working days.

**System-Level Capacity Failures**

The AER revealed profound systemic capacity constraints in the colorectal clinic:

- Annual capacity: 400 new first specialist assessments
- As of 7 March 2025: 196 new patients awaiting assessment (approximately six months of full-time work)
- 517 additional patients waiting for follow-up appointments, consuming the majority of available clinic time
- Semi-urgent patients were waiting up to 14 times longer than the Ministry of Health's recommended six-week wait time

No systematic monitoring existed to identify high-risk patients on the waitlist who might have significant pathology. The mismatch between capacity and demand had not been mitigated by any safety-netting mechanism.

**Cancer Discovery (June 2024)**

While awaiting his sigmoidoscopy, [Mr B]'s kidney function deteriorated. A CT scan on 20 June 2024, ordered by the nephrology service, revealed a rectosigmoid tumour with regional lymphadenopathy, probable hepatic metastases, and tumour obstruction of his right kidney and urinary tract.

A second general surgery referral was generated on 25 June 2024 and triaged as urgent. Colonoscopy occurred on 4 July 2024; surgical review followed on 18 July 2024 — 144 working days after the December referral, representing an 88-working-day excess beyond the appropriate 56-working-day standard.

[Mr B] was diagnosed with stage 4 colorectal cancer — transmural invasion with distant spread — resulting in poor prognosis.

**Total Diagnostic Delay**

From [Mr B]'s initial GP presentation in October 2023 to cancer diagnosis in June 2024 was approximately nine months. Contributing factors at each stage compounded the delay:

1. **October 2023**: Referral declined without clarification, no alternative pathway offered
2. **December 2023**: Administrative error extended triage timeframe by ~13 weeks
3. **Throughout**: No safety-netting mechanism to identify deteriorating waitlisted patients
4. **System-wide**: Colorectal clinic operating at 14x over the recommended wait time for semi-urgent cases

## Gold Standard Findings

### Contributing Factors Identified in Source

- Initial referral missing key alarm symptoms (bowel habit change not documented by GP)
- Gastroenterology declined referral without seeking symptom clarification from GP
- No direct referral transfer from gastroenterology to general surgery
- Administrative error: 120-working-day treat-by date set instead of 56-working-day semi-urgent standard
- No systematic monitoring of high-risk patients on surgical waitlist
- Colorectal clinic capacity severely below demand: 196 new patients plus 517 follow-ups
- Semi-urgent patients waiting up to 14 times the recommended Ministry of Health wait time
- No mechanism to identify patients on waitlist whose condition was deteriorating

### Root Causes Identified in Source

- Fundamental capacity-demand mismatch in colorectal surgical services not mitigated by monitoring or safety-netting
- Absence of systematic high-risk patient identification on waitlists
- Administrative processes allowing triage errors without correction mechanisms
- No direct referral transfer protocols between gastroenterology and surgical services

### Recommendations from Source

- Enhanced gastroenterology triage protocols requiring symptom clarification before declining referrals
- Dedicated colorectal clinics to address backlog patients
- Direct referral transfer mechanisms between services
- Standardised referral templates emphasising alarm symptoms
- Rapid-access sigmoidoscopy clinics for high-risk patients
- Administrative process mapping to eliminate inefficiencies
- Systematic waitlist risk monitoring
