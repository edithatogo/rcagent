# Case 05: Failure to Protect Patient from Repeated Inappropriate Behaviour by Co-Patient

**Source**: HDC NZ Decision 22HDC00290 (Deputy Health and Disability Commissioner, 2 February 2026)
**Event Type**: Failure to provide safe environment / patient safety
**SAC Equivalent**: SAC 2 (significant psychological harm — PTSD, early discharge)
**Difficulty**: 1 — Simple

## Case Narrative

[Mrs B], an elderly woman recovering from leg amputation surgery at [Hospital X] between 30 January and 11 February 2022, was repeatedly subjected to inappropriate behaviour by a confused male co-patient on the ward. Over a 10-day period, despite staff implementing escalating interventions, [Mrs B] was touched while sleeping, watched during personal care, subjected to sexual comments, and had her belongings interfered with. She developed post-traumatic stress disorder and was discharged early to avoid continued distress. The Deputy Health and Disability Commissioner found [Health Organisation A] (Health NZ) breached the Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers' Rights.

**The Admitted Behaviour (1–10 February 2022)**

Documented incidents involving the male co-patient occurred on 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, and 10 February 2022. The family reported more incidents occurred than were formally recorded. The behaviour included:

- Touching [Mrs B] while she was sleeping
- Entering and remaining in [Mrs B]'s bay during personal care
- Making sexual comments
- Going through [Mrs B]'s personal belongings

**Initial Staff Responses (1–3 February 2022)**

Between 1 and 3 February, staff implemented an initial set of responses:
- Medication adjustments for the male patient
- Sensor mat monitoring
- Additional nursing time allocated
- Alarm bracelet fitted
- Regular checks and verbal redirection

These measures were ineffective in preventing continued incidents.

**Escalated Responses (7–10 February 2022)**

By 7 February, escalating responses included:
- 24/7 one-on-one healthcare assistant (from 7 February)
- Psychiatric consultation for the male patient
- Security guard posted from 10 February
- Private room offered to [Mrs B] (declined)

Despite these measures, incidents continued. [Mrs B] chose to be discharged early on 11 February rather than remain in the ward environment.

**Impact on [Mrs B]**

[Mrs B] experienced:
- Disrupted sleep throughout her admission
- Distress affecting her surgical recovery
- Development of post-traumatic stress disorder
- Subsequent fear of returning to hospital for follow-up care

[Mr A], [Mrs B]'s son, filed the complaint on her behalf.

**Key Issues Identified**

The Deputy Commissioner's investigation identified three primary failures:

1. **Ineffective interventions**: While safety measures were implemented, they were ineffective in preventing the behaviour from recurring over 10 days.

2. **Inadequate instructions to support staff**: Instructions provided to healthcare assistants and security staff were unclear or incomplete, reducing their effectiveness.

3. **Inaccurate documentation**: Incidents were not accurately documented in clinical notes, meaning the full pattern of behaviour was not captured in the formal record.

**Contested Issue — Ward Separation**

[Health Organisation A] argued that resourcing constraints prevented moving the male patient to an alternative ward or environment. The Deputy Commissioner acknowledged these resourcing realities while finding that [Mrs B]'s rights were nonetheless breached — specifically the right to have services provided in a manner minimising harm and optimising quality of life (Right 4(4)).

**Code Breach Finding**

The Deputy Commissioner found [Health Organisation A] breached Right 4(4) of the Code on the grounds that:

- Safety measures were implemented but remained ineffective across 10 days
- Instructions to support staff were unclear or incomplete
- Incidents were not accurately documented

The decision noted that while [Health Organisation A] acted in good faith and escalated responses progressively, the standard of care nonetheless fell short of [Mrs B]'s entitlement to a safe environment during her recovery.

**Context — Ward Safety Systems**

The investigation highlighted a gap in [Hospital X]'s protocols for managing situations where a confused, agitated patient poses an ongoing and documented risk to another patient's physical and psychological safety. The response relied on individual clinical judgement and case-by-case escalation rather than a defined protocol specifying at what point environmental separation becomes mandatory.

## Gold Standard Findings

### Contributing Factors Identified in Source

- Multiple documented incidents over 10 days despite progressive staff responses
- Safety measures (sensor mat, alarm bracelet, additional nursing time) ineffective in preventing behaviour
- Instructions provided to one-on-one healthcare assistant and security staff unclear or incomplete
- Incidents not accurately documented in clinical notes — full pattern not captured
- Private room for [Mrs B] declined; ward separation not achieved
- Male patient not transferred despite ongoing demonstrated inability to manage behaviour on ward
- Resourcing constraints limiting ward reconfiguration options

### Root Causes Identified in Source

- Absence of protocol specifying when environmental separation of at-risk patients becomes mandatory
- Inadequate documentation standards for incidents of this nature
- Unclear instruction framework for support staff in behavioural management scenarios
- Insufficient physical environment options (same-gender bays, low-stimulation spaces)

### Recommendations from Source

- Written apology to [Mrs B] and [Mr A]
- Three-month action plan update to HDC
- Six-month anonymised case study presentation to clinical staff
- Enhanced incident reporting education
- Patient safety prompts in ward team meetings
- Training for security and support staff on behavioural risk management
- Same-gender bay implementation project
- Low-stimulation spaces for confused or agitated patients
- Front-door security system installation
