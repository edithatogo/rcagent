# Case 01: Failures in Acute Mental Health Care Following Multiple Declined Referrals

**Source**: HDC NZ Decision 21HDC00502 (Deputy Health and Disability Commissioner, 2 February 2026)
**Event Type**: Mental health — acute care failure
**SAC Equivalent**: SAC 1 (death)
**Difficulty**: 3 — Complex

## Case Narrative

[Mr C], a 26-year-old man, died not long after discharge from an NGO respite facility in February 2021, following a prolonged pattern of declined mental health referrals, inadequate risk assessment, and systemic failures in discharge planning. The Health and Disability Commissioner found that [Health Organisation A] (Health NZ Te Matau a Māui Hawke's Bay) breached the Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers' Rights.

**Background — Failed Referral Pathway (October 2019 – June 2020)**

In October 2019, [Mr C]'s GP made the first written referral to the Mental Health and Addictions Service (MHAS) requesting psychiatric assessment for ADHD. MHAS requested additional information, which was never provided, and the referral lapsed without action.

In March 2020, [Mr C] presented to MHAS with suicidal ideation and was assessed under the Mental Health (Compulsory Assessment and Treatment) Act 1992. A psychiatrist determined he was not actively suicidal and recommended PHO counselling. That same month, [Mr C]'s GP submitted a second referral, which MHAS addressed by telephone. MHAS again recommended PHO counselling and closed the referral.

In May 2020, the GP's third written referral requested assessment for bipolar disorder and consideration of mood-stabilising medication. MHAS declined, citing the March 2020 assessment findings. PHO counselling remained the recommendation.

In June 2020, [Mr C]'s GP called MHAS reporting psychotic symptoms — auditory and visual hallucinations. MHAS staff suggested [Mr C] "self-present" rather than arranging a formal referral, and recommended addiction services as "the most appropriate course." [Mr C] did not present, and no further contact occurred for approximately eight months.

**Crisis Presentation (17 February 2021)**

At 3:45 PM on 17 February 2021, [Mr C]'s counsellor called the Emergency Mental Health Service (EMHS) reporting that [Mr C] had expressed specific plans to end his life, with a history of self-harm attempts, low mood, and alcohol and cannabis use. Nursing staff advised the counsellor to contact Police and documented: "await further contact from Counsellor, Police, or Mr C."

At approximately 4:00 PM, [Mr C] presented to the Emergency Department. Triage staff confirmed his stated desire to end his life and his specific plan.

At approximately 5:30 PM, [Social Worker H] conducted the EMHS assessment. [Mr C] expressed worry that his drug-use history would prevent him accessing mental health support — [Social Worker H] documented that "[Mr C] could pose a risk to himself if he felt that his needs were not being addressed appropriately." Following consultation with the on-call psychiatrist, inpatient admission was not indicated, but "a more in-depth assessment" was considered necessary given his history and suicidal ideation.

**Respite Admission (17–19 February 2021)**

At approximately 7:30 PM on 17 February, [Mr C] was admitted to an NGO respite facility. The plan included multidisciplinary team meeting (MDTM) discussion, psychiatrist review of diagnosis and medication, and liaison with previous providers.

On the morning of 18 February, [Psychiatrist J] wrote to [Mr C]'s GP noting that [Mr C] was prescribed methylphenidate and diazepam, had previously injected methylphenidate intravenously, and currently used alcohol and cannabis. [Psychiatrist J] stated that methylphenidate is contraindicated in active substance misuse and "strongly recommended" cessation of methylphenidate, benzodiazepines, and other drugs with abuse potential. This communication was not part of any MDTM meeting and was not documented as such.

That day, [Mr C] had a phone consultation with his GP and was "upset to learn of the changes to his medication." Following this call, [Mr C] threatened self-harm. [RN G] saw him that evening and informed him his case would be discussed in an MDTM the following morning regarding a potential psychiatric review, but assessment was "not guaranteed."

On 19 February, [Mr C] was discharged after a two-night stay. Discharge reasons were not recorded contemporaneously. The discharge plan included contacting his GP regarding medication, daily phone welfare checks over the weekend, and EMHS contacting Community Mental Health for historical information. No written safety plan was provided. [Mr C]'s whānau were not consulted. No further mental health care appointments were arranged.

[Mr C] saw his GP on his discharge day. During this consultation, the GP called [Psychiatrist J], who stated "[Mr C] is at risk of injecting it." This conversation was not documented by [Psychiatrist J]. [Mr C] reported feeling "not properly assessed." The GP documented [Mr C] was agitated, discussed self-harm, and "calmed down a bit" after discussion. The GP prescribed diazepam "to settle him."

[Mr C] subsequently stayed at a friend's house and died not long afterward.

**Expert Opinion**

Independent psychiatrist [Dr A] identified multiple departures from accepted standards:

- **No in-person psychiatric review during respite** (moderate departure): [Mr C] received no psychiatric assessment during his two-night stay, missing an opportunity to reconsider inpatient admission, discuss medication changes, and address his specific concerns about substance-use history as a barrier to care.

- **Medication management** (moderate departure): The decision to cease methylphenidate and diazepam without direct discussion with [Mr C] and without a holistic review of his current high self-harm risk was inappropriate. While cessation was likely clinically appropriate long-term, the immediate priority was management of urgent self-harm risk.

- **Risk assessment inadequacy**: [Dr A] was "highly critical" of inadequate consideration of self-harm risk. More effort addressed addiction issues than "the present and urgent issue of high risk of self-harm."

- **Discharge and follow-up arrangements** (severe departure): No appointment scheduled; weekend welfare calls described as "vague"; no evidence of community mental health team contact; no management plan formulated with [Mr C]. Expected practice involves identifying and addressing risk factors, formulating management plans with patient involvement and whānau, and handing care to an appropriate team before discharge.

## Gold Standard Findings

### Contributing Factors Identified in Source

- Multiple GP referrals to MHAS not actioned or declined, preventing early support access
- Pattern of clinicians prioritising [Mr C]'s addiction history over expressed mental health needs
- [Mr C]'s own concern that drug-use history would prevent him accessing mental health support
- Absence of documented MDTM discussion, handover records, and information provided to the psychiatrist
- Lack of consistent risk assessment frameworks across multiple assessments
- Insufficient communication with whānau and GP regarding [Mr C]'s preceding months of presentation
- Absence of standard operating procedures for assessment standards, recovery plan documentation, and crisis team follow-up
- Clinicians unable to access cross-district health records, primary health records, or prescribing information
- Unclear staff roles and responsibilities regarding NGO respite facility entry and exit
- Adverse Event Review took more than four years to complete after the events

### Root Causes Identified in Source

- Systemic policy and procedural deficits not supporting safe decision-making, risk management, discharge planning, and documentation
- Inadequate assessment content standards and frameworks (risking overlooked information during risk evaluation)
- Absence of SOPs for acute mental health services ensuring recovery plan documentation and follow-up
- Inadequate whānau contact and involvement policies

### Recommendations from Source

- Formal written apology from [Psychiatrist J] and [Health Organisation A] to [Mr C]'s whānau
- Six-month update on change implementation (staff training, education)
- Report on referral criteria and pathways for psychotic symptoms and co-occurring addiction/mental health conditions
- Psychiatric review before significant medication changes; written safety plans before discharge
- MDTM documentation standards; Safeside Suicide risk framework
- Referral to Medical Council of NZ regarding [Psychiatrist J]
