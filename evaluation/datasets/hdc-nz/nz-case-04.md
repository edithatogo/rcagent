# Case 04: Neonatal Death Following Inadequate Monitoring During Nurse Break

**Source**: HDC NZ Decision 20HDC01313 (Health and Disability Commissioner, 23 February 2026)
**Event Type**: Surgical/perinatal — neonatal monitoring failure
**SAC Equivalent**: SAC 1 (death)
**Difficulty**: 2 — Moderate

## Case Narrative

[Baby A], born at 35+3 weeks gestation weighing 2,530 g via emergency caesarean section, died approximately 50 hours after birth from milk aspiration with airway obstruction. The Health and Disability Commissioner found that [Health Organisation A] (Health NZ) breached the Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers' Rights by failing to ensure [Baby A] was monitored for over an hour while the responsible bureau nurse was on a break, and through a lack of teamwork between nursing and midwifery staff.

**Birth and Initial Postnatal Management (Day 1–2)**

[Baby A] was initially placed in the Maternal Complex Care Area (MCCA) with normal Apgar scores (9/9) and satisfactory cord blood gas results. A nasogastric feeding plan was established at 60 mL/kg/day (19 mL every 3 hours). By Day 2 morning, multiple vomiting episodes were documented following feeds.

At 10:30 AM on Day 2, consultant neonatologist [Dr C] reviewed [Baby A], noting normal observations and low withdrawal scores. Vomiting continued throughout the day. At 4:00 PM, nasal flaring was documented before transfer to the postnatal ward.

**Respiratory Deterioration and Tube Management (Night of Day 2–3)**

At 10:30 PM, a NICU nurse practitioner reviewed [Baby A] after midwives noted increased work of breathing and tachypnoea. The nasogastric tube through the right nostril was identified as potentially obstructing breathing. The tube was removed, the nasal passage suctioned, and an orogastric tube inserted instead. [Baby A]'s breathing improved markedly.

On Day 3, Paediatric Registrar [Dr E] examined [Baby A] at 10:40 AM, documenting improvement. [Baby A] was feeding orally via supply line and tolerating feeds well across multiple feed times. That day, jaundice was noted (serum bilirubin 198) and phototherapy was commenced in an incubator with biliblanket and overhead light.

**Evening Day 3 — Feeding Tube and Consent**

That evening, a feeding assessment noted [Baby A] was clenching without suckling, with milk pooling in the mouth. The orogastric tube was replaced at 10:30 PM. Conflicting accounts exist regarding consent: [RN D] documented that [Ms A] agreed to the orogastric tube replacement; [Ms A] reported not feeling informed about the medical necessity and not being given meaningful options.

**The Fatal Night (Night of Day 3–4)**

At 11:20 PM, [Baby A] was noted as stirring and ready to feed. Clinical notes documented normal observations and a 23 mL formula feed that was tolerated.

At approximately 12:15 AM, [RN D] — a bureau (agency) nurse — commenced a one-hour break. Conflicting accounts were provided regarding the handover: [RN D] stated she specifically requested [Baby A] be checked during her absence; midwifery staff reported [RN D] indicated her patients were adequately cared for and would not require attention. Neither account was documented.

At the time of the break, [Baby A] had multiple risk factors warranting close monitoring:
- Premature (35+3 weeks gestation)
- Orogastric tube in situ
- Receiving phototherapy in an incubator
- Being monitored for opioid withdrawal
- Mother sleeping heavily in the same room

[Baby A] was not checked by any staff member during [RN D]'s one-hour break.

**Discovery and Resuscitation**

At approximately 1:35 AM, [RN D] returned and found [Baby A] pale and vomited, with the orogastric tube protruding from the mouth. [Baby A] was apnoeic and floppy. [RN D] carried [Baby A] to the resuscitation area rather than immediately activating the emergency bell, calling for colleagues to activate the code.

An emergency code was called at 1:48 AM. Resuscitation was attempted for 28 minutes. Milk was noted extensively in the mouth and below the vocal cords at intubation. Resuscitation was ceased at 2:16 AM.

The probable cause of death was milk aspiration with airway obstruction.

**Expert Analysis**

Independent paediatrician [Dr M] found no departure from accepted practice regarding [Baby A]'s placement in the postnatal ward or the medical assessments. He raised concerns about initial feed volumes (potentially high for a late pre-term infant) but ultimately characterised this as within the range of clinical judgment at the time.

Independent nursing advisor [RN C] was critical of three aspects:

1. **Monitoring during break**: Given [Baby A]'s risk profile, [Baby A] "should have been checked at least once" during the one-hour break. She noted the ward workload was not high and would not have prevented this.

2. **Intentional rounding**: "The concept of intentional rounding is well established practice." [Baby A]'s cumulative risk factors (prematurity, orogastric tube, phototherapy, withdrawal monitoring) warranted regular interval checking regardless of handover communication.

3. **Teamwork and accountability**: [RN C] identified "a lack of teamwork and accountability demonstrated by the staff on the postnatal ward." A bureau nurse should work with a specified registered nurse contact who checks in about patients during the shift.

**Code Breach Finding**

The Commissioner found [Health Organisation A] breached Right 4(5) of the Code — the right to have services provided in a manner that minimises potential harm — by:

1. Failing to ensure [Baby A] was checked for over one hour while [RN D] was on break
2. An apparent lack of teamwork between nursing and midwifery staff

The Commissioner also identified "a clear breakdown in communication between [Ms A] and clinical staff... which resulted in [Ms A] not feeling appropriately informed about the requirement for a feeding tube."

## Gold Standard Findings

### Contributing Factors Identified in Source

- [Baby A] not checked for over one hour during bureau nurse's break despite multiple risk factors
- Communication breakdown between [RN D] and midwifery staff regarding monitoring expectations during break
- Bureau nurse working without designated RN contact who would check in on her patients
- No documented handover instructions regarding monitoring frequency
- Monitoring plan not accounting for cumulative risk factors (prematurity, orogastric tube, phototherapy, withdrawal monitoring, sleeping mother)
- Feeding tube replacement without adequate parental informed consent process
- Charge Midwife Coordinator not aware of feeding tube decision or offered leadership support

### Root Causes Identified in Source

- Lack of teamwork and accountability culture between nursing and midwifery staff groups
- Bureau nurses not integrated with designated nurse contact for patient safety oversight
- Individual clinical judgement insufficient to override monitoring protocol gaps given risk profile
- Absence of intentional rounding framework applied to high-risk neonates

### Recommendations from Source

- Written apology to [Ms A] and [Mr B]
- Health NZ to consider expert advisor suggestions and report within six months
- Designated transitional care unit for late pre-term babies (implemented)
- Safety briefings at each shift start (implemented)
- Kōrero Mai escalation pathway for concerns (implemented)
- Clinical Midwife Manager on every shift (implemented)
